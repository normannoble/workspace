Progression Guide: IC1 to IC2

L&D Coordinator to L&D Specialist

Overview

The transition from IC1 (Associate) to IC2 (Professional) represents the shift from learning the craft through coordination and support to independently designing and delivering training programs. This is the foundational career transition in Learning & Development—moving from ensuring programs run smoothly to creating the learning experiences themselves.

This transition typically takes 1-3 years at IC1 and requires demonstrating that you can apply instructional design principles, facilitate learning sessions, and own program areas with increasing independence.

The Core Shift

IC1 (Associate) IC2 (Professional)
Coordinates training logistics—rooms, materials, scheduling Designs and delivers training programs for assigned areas
Administers the LMS and maintains learner records Creates e-learning content using authoring tools
Prepares materials and sends participant communications Facilitates workshops and training sessions with growing confidence
Compiles post-training evaluation data for the team Analyzes evaluation data and makes program improvements based on findings
Supports program delivery managed by others Owns defined program areas and manages the content lifecycle
Builds foundational understanding of adult learning Applies instructional design principles to structure learning objectives and activities
Success = reliable coordination with decreasing oversight Success = consistent delivery of well-designed learning experiences

The fundamental question changes from "Am I keeping training operations running smoothly?" to "Am I creating learning experiences that develop people effectively?"**

Key Skills to Develop

1. Instructional Design

At IC1, you develop an awareness of adult learning principles by observing how programs are designed. At IC2, you apply instructional design principles to create training programs with clear learning objectives, appropriate activities, and logical content flow.

What this looks like:

  • You structure learning objectives that are specific, measurable, and aligned with the performance outcomes the program targets
  • You design activities that engage adult learners—incorporating practice, reflection, and application rather than passive information delivery
  • You sequence content logically, building from foundational concepts to complex application
  • You create e-learning modules using authoring tools that are engaging, accessible, and aligned with organizational learning standards

How to develop this:

  • Study instructional design fundamentals—ADDIE, SAM, or equivalent frameworks—through courses, books, or professional development
  • Ask to co-design a program with a senior L&D team member, taking ownership of specific modules while receiving feedback on your design choices
  • Practice writing learning objectives for existing programs you coordinate—compare yours with the actual objectives and discuss differences with the designer
  • Build proficiency in at least one e-learning authoring tool (Articulate, Captivate, or equivalent) through hands-on practice projects

2. Facilitation & Delivery

At IC1, you support facilitators and observe how sessions are run. At IC2, you facilitate workshops and training sessions yourself, managing group dynamics and adapting delivery in real time.

What this looks like:

  • You lead standard training sessions and workshops for varied professional audiences with confidence
  • You manage group dynamics effectively—drawing out quiet participants, managing dominant voices, and keeping sessions on track
  • You adapt delivery when participants need more or less depth, adjusting pace and emphasis without losing the learning objectives
  • You receive consistently positive feedback from learners on your facilitation effectiveness

How to develop this:

  • Volunteer to facilitate low-stakes portions of existing programs—an icebreaker, a review session, or a Q&A segment—and build up from there
  • Ask a senior facilitator to observe your sessions and provide specific, actionable feedback on your delivery
  • Practice facilitation skills in any context available—team meetings, lunch-and-learns, onboarding orientations
  • Study facilitation techniques: how to ask effective questions, manage energy in the room, and handle difficult participants

3. Learning Evaluation & Improvement

At IC1, you compile evaluation data into summary reports. At IC2, you analyze evaluation results, identify what's working and what isn't, and make concrete improvements to programs based on the data.

What this looks like:

  • Post-training evaluations are consistently collected and analyzed, with clear reports identifying strengths and areas for improvement
  • You move beyond satisfaction surveys toward evidence that learning is being applied on the job
  • Evaluation data drives concrete changes in program design and delivery, creating a visible cycle of iteration and improvement
  • You flag trends and anomalies in completion and participation data, providing context for why patterns might be occurring

How to develop this:

  • Go beyond compiling evaluation scores—start writing analysis summaries that interpret the data and recommend specific program changes
  • For a program you coordinate, track whether participants actually apply what they learned—talk to their managers a month after training
  • Propose one specific improvement to an existing program based on evaluation data and implement it with guidance from a senior team member
  • Learn about Kirkpatrick's evaluation model and begin thinking about how to measure beyond Level 1 (satisfaction) toward Level 2 (knowledge acquisition)

4. Stakeholder Communication & Needs Gathering

At IC1, you communicate about logistics—schedules, materials, and enrollment. At IC2, you communicate about program substance—timelines, participation, outcomes—and gather information from stakeholders that improves program relevance.

What this looks like:

  • Program stakeholders are kept informed about timelines, participation, and outcomes through consistent, clear communication
  • You gather meaningful input from managers and subject matter experts that improves program content
  • You develop the skill of asking questions that distinguish between stated requests and underlying performance gaps
  • You build productive working relationships with facilitators, vendors, and cross-functional partners

How to develop this:

  • Start asking "why" questions when stakeholders request training—what performance gap are they trying to close? What would success look like?
  • Practice communicating program outcomes, not just logistics—instead of "the session is scheduled," try "here's what participants will be able to do after this session"
  • Build relationships with at least two subject matter experts who can help you understand the domains your programs serve
  • Seek feedback from stakeholders on the quality and usefulness of your communications—are they getting what they need?

Common Pitfalls

Staying in coordination mode

The biggest risk is continuing to do IC1 work at a higher quality rather than developing IC2 capabilities. If you're the best coordinator on the team but haven't designed or facilitated a program, you're not progressing. Actively seek design and delivery opportunities.

Designing content in isolation

New designers sometimes create programs based on their own assumptions rather than learner needs and stakeholder input. Always validate your design choices with the people who will use the training and the managers who need the performance outcomes.

Avoiding facilitation opportunities

Facilitation is uncomfortable at first, and it's easy to find reasons to stay behind the scenes. But IC2 requires facilitation capability. Start small, get feedback, and build confidence through practice—not avoidance.

Equating activity with effectiveness

At IC1, success is measured by whether things happen (sessions run, records are accurate). At IC2, success is measured by whether learning happens. Don't confuse a well-organized program with an effective one—always ask whether participants are actually learning and applying.

How to Demonstrate Readiness

Promotion to IC2 requires sustained demonstration of professional-level capability over 6+ months. Here's how to build your case:

Build a Track Record

  • Document training programs or modules you have designed, including the learning objectives, design rationale, and evaluation results
  • Collect feedback from facilitation sessions you've led—both learner evaluations and observer feedback from senior team members
  • Show examples of program improvements you identified through evaluation data and implemented
  • Demonstrate growing independence in managing assigned program areas with reduced oversight

Self-Assessment Questions

  1. Can I design a training program from scratch with clear learning objectives, appropriate activities, and logical content flow?
  2. Am I comfortable facilitating workshops and training sessions for professional audiences, managing group dynamics effectively?
  3. Do I analyze evaluation data and use it to make concrete improvements to programs—not just compile reports?
  4. Can I gather meaningful input from stakeholders and subject matter experts that improves program relevance?
  5. Am I managing assigned program areas with increasing independence, or do I still need frequent guidance on design and delivery decisions?
  6. Do I understand adult learning principles well enough to explain why my design choices work?

If you can answer "yes" to most of these consistently over 6+ months, you're likely ready for IC2.

Typical Timeline

Most L&D professionals progress from IC1 to IC2 in 1-3 years. This timeline varies based on the breadth of design and facilitation opportunities available, the quality of mentorship, and the individual's pace of skill development. The transition is faster for those who actively seek stretch assignments beyond coordination and invest in building instructional design and facilitation skills through practice and professional development.

Remember: IC2 is about creating learning experiences, not just coordinating them. You need to demonstrate that you can design, deliver, and improve training programs—not just ensure they run smoothly.
