HR Management Career Progression

Management Track Overview

Introduction

This document outlines career progression on the Management track for HR leaders. Whether you're considering your first management role or are an experienced manager looking to grow, this guide will help you understand what success looks like at each level, how promotions work, and what it takes to develop as a people leader within the HR function.

Our approach to leveling is outcome-based, not task-based. Each level describes what success looks like—the desired end states—rather than a checklist of activities. This means you're evaluated on impact and outcomes, not just what you do day-to-day.

Core Philosophy

Management levels measure people leadership capability and effectiveness, not organizational scale. Like IC levels, they follow a progression of learning, doing, and teaching. Scale (team size, organizational complexity) is a context factor that influences the difficulty of operating at each level, but does not define the level itself.

As you progress through management levels, the fundamental question changes:

  • M1: "Am I learning to be an effective manager?"
  • M2: "Am I operating effectively without oversight?"
  • M3: "Am I developing excellent managers and shaping how HR management works?"

Plateauing is normal and healthy. Many HR managers have successful, fulfilling careers at M1 or M2. Not everyone needs to reach the top level, and the happiest managers are those who find fulfillment in their work, regardless of title.

The Three Management Levels at a Glance

Level Title Primary Focus Core Question
M1 HR Manager Learning management fundamentals Am I learning to be an effective manager?
M2 Senior HR Manager Operating independently with confidence Am I effective without oversight?
M3 Head of HR Developing managers and shaping the HR function Am I developing excellent managers?

M1: HR Manager

Learning Management Fundamentals

The HR Manager is a first-level people leader responsible for building and leading a small HR team (typically 3-6 people), usually within a single discipline or a focused subset of HR operations. This role marks the transition from individual contribution to people leadership, with a focus on developing foundational management skills while maintaining deep HR domain expertise.

What Success Looks Like

  • A team that consistently delivers quality HR work and meets commitments, with clear ownership and accountability across assignments
  • Team members who understand expectations, receive regular feedback, and are developing their skills within their HR discipline
  • A healthy team culture with psychological safety, where people feel comfortable raising concerns, surfacing compliance risks, and asking for help
  • Basic management practices executed consistently: regular 1:1s, timely feedback, clear goal-setting, and fair workload distribution
  • Domain credibility maintained through direct contribution to HR programs, policy interpretation, and hands-on work when the team needs support
  • HR programs, cycles, and deliverables completed on time with appropriate quality, with early communication when timelines are at risk

Key Characteristics

  • First-time manager or early in management journey
  • Learning how to conduct effective 1:1s, give feedback, and manage performance in a function where interpersonal sensitivity is high
  • Building trust and rapport with direct reports while remaining hands-on in the domain
  • Needs regular coaching and guidance from senior HR leaders
  • Handles straightforward performance and interpersonal issues; escalates complex situations with context
  • Translates HR function goals into team-level objectives

Typical Experience

5-8 years of professional HR experience across one or more disciplines. First formal management role, or less than 2 years in a management capacity. Usually promoted from IC2-IC3 (occasionally IC4).

M2: Senior HR Manager

Operating Independently with Confidence

The Senior HR Manager is a proficient people leader who manages with independence and confidence across multiple HR disciplines. This role may involve leading a larger HR team (8-15 people), managing M1 HR Managers, or owning significant HR programs that span Talent Acquisition, HR Operations, HRBP, L&D, and Compensation & Benefits. The emphasis is on operating effectively without significant oversight from senior leadership.

What Success Looks Like

  • HR teams that consistently deliver high-quality work across disciplines with strong culture and low regrettable attrition
  • Team members and M1 managers showing meaningful career growth, with clear development plans and active coaching
  • Complex people situations handled independently: performance issues, conflicts, retention risks, difficult conversations, and the unique challenges of managing HR professionals
  • Sound people decisions made confidently: hiring strong HR talent, compensation, promotions, and terminations within the HR function
  • Significant HR programs owned end-to-end with clear accountability, consistent quality, and measurable outcomes
  • Effective coordination across HR disciplines—ensuring recruiting, onboarding, HRBP support, L&D programming, and compensation practices work as an integrated system
  • Strong partnerships with business leaders built on trust, transparency, and reliable delivery of HR services

Key Shift from M1

The fundamental question changes from "Am I learning to be an effective manager?" to "Am I operating effectively without oversight?" At M1, you handle straightforward situations and escalate complex ones. At M2, you handle the full range of people situations with confidence and sound judgment. Your manager trusts you to operate autonomously. You also shift from managing within a single discipline to coordinating across multiple HR disciplines and developing M1 managers into effective leaders.

Typical Experience

8-12 years of professional HR experience across multiple disciplines. 3-5 years in HR management with demonstrated growth in capability and scope. Progression from M1 typically takes 3-5 years.

M3: Head of HR

Developing Managers and Shaping the HR Function

The Head of HR is a senior leader responsible for a significant portion of or the entire HR function, managing M1 and M2 HR managers and shaping how HR operates at organizational scale. This role reports to executive leadership (CHRO, CPO, or CEO in smaller organizations) and contributes directly to company strategy and the overall people agenda.

What Success Looks Like

  • Multiple HR teams delivering consistently against strategic objectives with coordinated execution across all five disciplines
  • HR managers (M1s and M2s) who are growing in capability as people leaders—not just effective HR practitioners who happen to manage
  • Delegation that develops managers while ensuring quality outcomes—genuine development of leadership judgment, strategic thinking, and cross-discipline fluency
  • A management culture within HR with shared practices, standards, and expectations that reflect the same rigor the function asks of the rest of the organization
  • Bench strength and succession planning—the HR function is not dependent on any single manager
  • HR standards and practices adopted across all disciplines that measurably improve service quality, employee experience, and compliance posture
  • Cross-discipline integration that creates compounding value—recruiting feeding into strong onboarding, HRBP insights informing compensation strategy, HR Operations enabling it all with reliable infrastructure
  • Forward-looking planning that anticipates organizational needs 6-18 months ahead—workforce planning, capability gaps, compliance changes, and technology investments

Key Shift from M2

The fundamental question changes from "Am I operating effectively without oversight?" to "Am I developing excellent managers and shaping how HR management works?" At M2, your success is measured by your team's performance. At M3, your success is measured by your managers' growth and the HR function's overall capability. You operate primarily through others, shaping how the entire HR function delivers against the company's people agenda.

Typical Experience

10-15+ years of professional HR experience across multiple disciplines. 5-8+ years in HR management, including experience managing other managers. Progression from M2 typically takes 4-8+ years, and M3 positions are limited.

How Promotions Work

Core Principles

Promotions are earned, not given based on time. Time at level is necessary but not sufficient. You must demonstrate sustained performance at the next level—promotions recognize impact already being delivered, not potential.

Promotion criteria are cumulative. You must continue meeting all criteria from previous levels. Higher levels build on the foundation of previous levels.

What We Look For

  • Current Level Mastery: Consistently operating at the top end of your current level
  • Next Level Performance: Already demonstrating success outcomes of the next level for 6-12+ months
  • Readiness for Continued Growth: Trajectory suggests continued growth beyond the next level

Typical Timelines

Transition Typical Time Note
M1 → M2 3-5 years Becoming proficient at management across disciplines
M2 → M3 4-8+ years Senior leadership is highly competitive

Note: These are guidelines, not guarantees. Faster progressions are possible with exceptional performance; slower progressions are also normal.

Common Career Patterns

Healthy Distribution

In a healthy organization, the level distribution for HR managers looks something like this:

  • M1: 60-70% of managers (most managers)
  • M2: 25-35% of managers (experienced managers)
  • M3: <10% of all managers (senior leadership)

Key Messages

  • Plateauing at M1 or M2 represents strong career success. Not everyone needs to reach M3.
  • Career satisfaction comes from mastery and impact, not just titles.
  • Compensation can grow within levels. Sustained excellence at your current level is recognized.
  • Different levels require different skills. Moving up isn't just about getting better—it's about developing genuinely new capabilities.

Transitions Between Tracks

Management and IC careers are not one-way doors:

  • M1 (1-2 years) → IC3: Relatively straightforward return to IC track within your HR discipline
  • M1 (3+ years) or M2 → IC3/IC4: May need to rebuild depth in your discipline, but management experience is valued
  • IC2-IC3 → M1: Most common entry point into HR management (75% of cases)
  • IC4 → M1: Occasional (20% of cases)—senior IC taking on a team

Moving between tracks is valid and respected. The key is finding work that aligns with your interests and strengths. HR management carries the unique dimension of managing professionals who are themselves people experts—this requires particular self-awareness and authenticity.

Your Career Development

Taking Ownership

Your career development is primarily your responsibility. While your manager will support you, you should take initiative to:

  • Understand what success looks like at your current level and the next
  • Seek feedback proactively and act on it
  • Look for opportunities to demonstrate next-level capabilities
  • Have honest conversations with your manager about your career goals
  • Document your impact and contributions

Questions to Reflect On

  • Am I consistently demonstrating success at my current level?
  • What capabilities do I need to develop to reach the next level?
  • What opportunities can I seek to develop those capabilities?
  • Is the work at the next level genuinely interesting to me?
  • What does long-term career fulfillment look like for me?

Remember: A successful management career isn't about racing to the top—it's about finding work that challenges you, developing people effectively, and making a meaningful contribution. Whatever level you're at, focus on building excellent HR teams and growing in ways that align with your interests and strengths.
